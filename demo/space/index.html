160 made-up secrets were hidden among 2 million training stories, each repeated between 0 and 64 times. The attacker knows the sentence pattern and the name, but not the number, and asks each model for its 100 most likely 6-digit completions (a beam search over digits).

Can an attacker tell whether a story was in the training data? A plain "low loss means member" test barely works here. The strong version compares the model under attack with a reference model trained on different stories: if the target finds a story much easier than the reference does, it probably trained on it.

What's going on

Everything here was implemented from scratch for this project: the tokenizer, the 27.5M-parameter transformer, the training loop, differentially private training (DP-SGD) and its privacy accounting, and the attacks. PyTorch supplied tensors and gradients.

  • The undefended model memorizes repeated data. Secrets that appear 64 times are in the attacker's top 100 guesses 70% of the time.
  • Deduplication is free and stops secret extraction, because the 64 copies collapse into one. It does not stop membership inference.
  • DP-SGD stops both attacks, at a large cost in quality: try writing a story with it.

How this page works

The models were exported to ONNX and run in your browser with ONNX Runtime Web (WebAssembly). Their weights are stored at half precision to halve the download, and all arithmetic is full precision. The browser code is checked against the PyTorch originals: identical tokens, identical greedy stories, story losses within 0.002, and the same beam-search results. "Saved results" were computed with the same weights in PyTorch.

Data: TinyStories (Eldan & Li, 2023). Attacks follow Carlini et al. (2019, 2021) and Shokri et al. (2017); DP-SGD follows Abadi et al. (2016). Built with Claude Code as a coding assistant; I directed the design and experiments and reviewed the code and results.
